The question

What does the temperature record actually show?

Global warming is easier to understand when we look beyond a single year. NASA's GISTEMP v4 dataset measures global surface temperature anomalies: how much warmer or cooler each year was compared with the 1951-1980 average.

We examine the record from three perspectives: year-by-year change, a visual timeline of warm and cool years and decade-by-decade averages.

01 / ALTAIR · PYTHON

The long-term temperature trend

The annual line chart tracks global temperature anomalies from 1880 to 2025. Individual years fluctuate, but the long-term direction is unmistakably upward, especially after 1980.

Key insight: Year-to-year variability does not erase the sustained rise in global temperature.

02 / GOOGLE SHEETS

A century and a half in color

Each vertical stripe represents a year. Blue indicates a cooler-than-baseline year; red indicates a warmer-than-baseline year. The transition makes the long-term change visible at a glance.

Key insight: The concentration of red stripes in recent years illustrates how persistent warming has become.

03 / TABLEAU PUBLIC

Which decades were the warmest?

A decade-by-decade bar chart averages annual temperature anomalies to reduce short-term noise and compare longer periods. Recent decades stand out as much warmer than earlier ones.

Key insight: Warming persists when we average over decades, not just individual years.

The 2020s bar includes 2020–2025 only; it is not a full decade.

The conclusion

Different views. One consistent signal.

The line chart shows the annual rise, the warming stripes make the changing balance of cool and warm years immediately apparent and the decade comparison confirms that the pattern extends beyond short-term fluctuations.

Together, the three designs tell a consistent story: Earth's global average surface temperature has risen substantially since 1880.

Temperature anomalies are relative to NASA's 1951-1980 reference average, not absolute global temperatures.
